Dissertation

Nationalization in Sub-National Elections
Abstract

Scholars widely agree that American elections have become nationalized, but existing work treats nationalization as something that happens to elections rather than something political actors produce. This dissertation reframes nationalization as an active process of political communication. Campaigns, outside groups, and media elites supply nationalized messages, and voters accept, resist, or ignore those messages in ways that vary across individuals and subnational contexts.

First, I develop a definition of nationalized campaign strategy and rhetoric, drawing on Affective Intelligence Theory, issue ownership theory, and the campaign strategy literature. I link nationalized advertising to four campaign characteristics: electoral competitiveness, emotional appeal, advertising medium, and funding source. Comparing binary, ordinal, and ternary measures, I show that nationalization is partial rather than on-off, and that many campaigns run mixed-frame ads that blend national and local appeals.

Second, I develop a micro-level theory of how nationalized cues shape voters. Through agenda setting, these cues determine which issues voters use to evaluate subnational candidates. Through framing, they connect unfamiliar local races to familiar national partisan schemas. I argue that these cues carry information beyond partisanship, affect both mobilization and persuasion, and matter most for weak partisans and less politically sophisticated voters.

Working Papers

Disentangling Causal Mechanisms in Conjoint Experiments Using Mediation [arXiv]

With Michaël Aklin, Max Goplerud, and Nicole E. Pashley

Abstract

Conjoint experiments provide an attractive way to assess the role of multiple attributes simultaneously on decision-making. However, the randomization of multiple attributes prevents understanding the causal mechanisms that, critically, depend on the relationship between attributes -- e.g., how one attribute affects the respondent's belief as to another attribute. This is because conjoint experiments recover controlled effects whereas a substantively important estimand may be the total or indirect effect of one attribute. Unfortunately, existing experimental designs for conjoint experiments cannot estimate these effects. We provide an alternative framework that requires one additional, simple experiment to learn the relationship between attributes among respondents alongside the standard assumptions for causal mediation. Estimation of the relevant effects can be done in a doubly robust fashion using machine learning methods. We illustrate this by conducting a pre-registered experiment on candidate choice and disentangle the effect of different attributes by understanding their mediation through the candidate's party.
